50 Ways to Leave Another: creative and commercial treatment

The hook

A professional contract killer discovers that her lover has betrayed her with someone they both trusted. He knows exactly what she is capable of. Everyone expects revenge. Her most consequential choice is to refuse both retaliation and the assignment—and leave.

Core question: What happens when a person whose livelihood depends on control decides to stop allowing others to determine her choices?

Format and audience — proposal: a premium serialized thriller conceived as a limited series for adult audiences interested in psychological tension, intimate betrayal, agency and music-led storytelling. This is a positioning proposal, not evidence of audience demand, buyer interest, financing or a finalized episode order.

Creative authority and continuity

The current owner-supplied foundation establishes the profession, betrayal by a lover and shared trusted person, expected revenge, and eventual refusal of retaliation and the assignment. These are fictional narrative facts, not claims about real people.

Existing project materials explore a five-song EP and five-part music/story progression, the return of a title song in different production states and a studio as dramatic space. Those remain development references. They do not finalize this thriller's episode count, runtime, names, biographies, setting, secondary plots, on-screen collaborators or soundtrack obligations. No new finalized scripts or character biographies are invented here.

The story: reclaiming the decision

Foundation: the protagonist earns her living through control. Her lover knowingly risks a relationship with someone accustomed to decisive action. Betrayal makes revenge the anticipated response; the trusted third person's role makes the breach intimate rather than abstract.

Proposed dramatic movement: discovery makes a familiar solution available. As she tests what action would actually reclaim her life, retaliation begins to resemble another task organized around other people's choices. Refusing the assignment alongside the relationship makes the ending an active withdrawal of consent. The suspense comes from whether she can stop performing the role that everyone—including herself—expects.

Open development questions: what the assignment is, how it intersects with the relationship, who knows about the betrayal, which consequences follow refusal, and how the final departure is dramatized. These need the owner's acceptance and writers' work. The story must earn independence through choices, not suggest that violence is a universal response to betrayal. No diagnosis or real-life analogue is assigned to any character.

Autonomy, identity, the economics of intimacy and the difference between power and freedom provide the thematic frame. Psychological tension and consequence are the promise; specific action sequences, locations and production scale remain undecided.

Music as narrative

Creator account: an existing human-recorded song and its development helped reactivate the fictional storyline. The private source archive is not reproduced here. The public project also documents a Suno-assisted demo/interpretation; its exact version, inputs and applicable plan/workflow remain to be verified.

Proposal: allow one musical idea to change meaning as the protagonist changes her relationship to control. A sparse opening, an interrupted motif or a fuller later performance could distinguish anticipation from a choice actually lived. These are development options, not instructions to reproduce Paul Simon's recording or a named artist's style, voice or music.

The existing five-part map offers a useful working method: early version, inventory, fracture, witnessing, presence. A writers' room and music lead should test that progression against the current thriller foundation instead of treating five tracks as five mandatory episodes. Each composition, performance, arrangement, render and editorial cue needs its own version and permission record.

A potential original soundtrack could create music-first storytelling, featured recordings and separate screen/music releases. Sync opportunities are contingent on the buyer's specifications, authorized chain of title, actual versions and negotiated scope. The concept is not a cleared catalog submission. No music is included in this treatment or pitch PDF.

Potential Music & Screen Partnership — UMG / Drake Concept

This is an unsolicited hypothesis, not an approach made to an artist or an accepted relationship. Drake illustrates an established-artist role; another suitable artist or an independent team could fill it. No need for a “comeback,” reputational injury, motive or desire to participate is asserted.

Creative value to explore: a coherent narrative and music-development process could give an artist room to contribute original work to a screen property, with clear credit, approvals and fair licensing rather than a request to reproduce an existing hit. Audience re-engagement is a possible objective to research, not a promised result.

A separate production and review model

The show is presented here in the portfolio. The separate 50 Ways behind-the-scenes site preserves its development, learning and professional-review record. Participants work through how the project can be used, promoted and monetized: waterfall/cash timing, rights management, royalty collection, fair compensation and evidence-backed decisions. These remain reviewable questions rather than a certified or fully implemented business model.

Gods of Mayhem is the contributors, professionals, experienced practitioners and thought leaders who have the capacity to participate for the ecosystem's benefit. Their time and scope are chosen; contribution does not imply endorsement, a professional retainer or waived rights to compensation. The purpose is collaborative correction and better decisions.

Historical production details and the sanitized correspondence/cost summary belong in the separate learning case, not the show pitch. Private originals remain private.
