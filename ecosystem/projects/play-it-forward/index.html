PLAY IT FORWARD

What if the next era of music, games, stories and merch didn't require an independent creator to surrender their audience, rights or budget to a single platform?

We built a small, connected experiment: a public portfolio and ecosystem map, a human-led interactive FILTHY YUCK! story, optional routes to the band's comic and merch, and a practical AI Adventure School that documents how the work was made. It is a prototype for learning and review—not a proven business, official band activation or GTA VI partnership.

01 / The cultural moment

From the November 6 Bandcamp Friday to GTA VI's November 19 launch

November 6, 2026 was the most recent Bandcamp Friday. Bandcamp waived its revenue share for purchases that day; payment-processing fees still applied. GTA VI launched worldwide November 19, opening a new, highly visible era for interactive entertainment and music culture. These events are context—not affiliation, endorsement, or a claim that either platform needs our project.

Rather than treat them as unrelated headlines, we asked: How can an independent creator use a cultural moment to invite people into an owned, human-led creative experience—and turn that attention into durable relationships, learning and sustainable economics?

02 / The pitch

Don't sell the platform. Build the experience people want to pass forward.

PLAY IT FORWARD connects a band's real-world creative material to a playful, optional story. The story demonstrates that AI can support production without becoming the author: the human defines intention, reviews outputs, keeps or rejects suggestions, protects source rights and decides what ships.

Audience entry

Start with a shareable moment, a question or a choice—not a mandatory account, data capture or purchase.

Experience

Optional discovery

03 / Distribution waterfall

One story. Different channels. A deliberate next step.

Each channel has a distinct job. We do not duplicate the whole experience everywhere or depend on one algorithm or storefront.

  1. Owned hub — portfolio / PLAY IT FORWARD. Durable explanation, map and source links. Establish context and a clear invitation.
  2. Interactive destination — Pay It Forward. The story is the experience; social posts are invitations, not replacements.
  3. Training — AI Adventure School. Production workflow, rights-aware use, human review, cost questions and lessons. Turn the experiment into reusable capability.
  4. Short-form discovery — artist-owned social. Teaser, question, choice or behind-the-scenes clip. Link back to the owned experience; no copied funnel or fake urgency.
  5. Professional distribution — LinkedIn / portfolio. Frame the case around independent economics, systems design, accessibility, ownership and verifiable implementation. Invite reviewers and collaborators.
  6. Community and press — relevant fan, creator, game and music channels. Pitch a transparent experiment at a cultural moment. Respect community rules; no spam or implied affiliation.
  7. Commerce — creator's official store. Comic and merch remain optional destinations. Measure sales only through store reporting and with permission; no conversion claim is assumed.
  8. Feedback loop. Public GitHub issues and voluntary feedback identify corrections and next tests. Share outcomes and credit; retain source and creative authority.

Waterfall principle: create once with a durable source, adapt the invitation to each channel, and send people to the next meaningful step. The audience is not the product; the creative work and relationship are.

04 / The economic reality beneath the creative work

Survival is an administrative workload

For a person navigating assistance, medical issues or unstable income, “start a creative business” is not a neutral instruction. The work may be layered on top of housing recertification, benefits renewals, medical appointments, prior authorizations, documentation requests, transportation, caregiving, appeals and the risk of a missed deadline causing a loss of support.

HUD-assisted housing commonly requires periodic household income and composition reviews. A change in income can change rent contributions; reporting and recertification are not simply paperwork detached from a person's life. Medicaid renewals likewise require eligibility systems and enrollees to exchange information, and procedural disenrollment can happen even when someone remains eligible. KFF's analysis of the Medicaid unwinding documented millions of coverage losses and the complexity of renewal processes.

Time is a real cost

Calls, forms, collecting records, waiting, correcting mistakes, following up and appeals consume time and attention. Those hours compete with paid work, rest, treatment, caregiving and creative work.

Risk is asymmetric

A person with savings can absorb a delayed payment, failed project or unexpected expense. Someone relying on housing or medical support may face consequences that threaten stability, health or shelter.

Healthcare administration is not only the patient

The current healthcare model includes insurance eligibility, network limits, billing, prior authorization and provider-side paperwork. The AMA's 2025 physician survey reported practices averaging 40 prior authorizations per physician weekly and 13 hours of staff time per week spent on them—evidence of system-wide friction, not a direct measure of an individual patient's burden.

Capacity is variable

Medical conditions, disability, mental health, fatigue and unpredictable symptoms can make schedules and output fluctuate. A responsible creative workflow needs low-pressure participation, asynchronous access, no penalty for pauses and no assumption that people can work at a fixed pace.

Design consequence: the prototype should be free to explore, require no account, avoid collecting sensitive personal data, make commerce optional, keep feedback voluntary, and never promise that AI or creative entrepreneurship will replace benefits or solve structural poverty.

05 / Policy signal, fact-check and uncertainty

Project 2025: distinguish the blueprint, the campaign and enacted policy

Project 2025, led by the Heritage Foundation with a coalition of conservative organizations, published a policy blueprint for a future conservative administration. Its contributors included many former Trump administration officials and campaign or transition figures. Trump publicly distanced himself from it during the 2024 campaign. Since returning to office, however, his administration has pursued or implemented a number of proposals that overlap with the blueprint. That is documented policy overlap—not proof that Project 2025 alone caused the election result or that every proposal was adopted.

On social safety-net policy, the evidence is concrete. Project 2025 advocated Medicaid eligibility and financing changes, including work incentives, greater state flexibility and targeted limits. The 2025 reconciliation law signed July 4, 2025 includes Medicaid work and reporting requirements for certain expansion adults, changes financing and eligibility, and delays some administrative simplification rules. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the law's health provisions would reduce federal outlays by about $1.1 trillion over ten years and increase the uninsured population by 10 million by 2034; those are projections, not observed outcomes.

Project 2025 also proposed major HUD changes, including greater state control and rental-assistance restrictions. The administration's 2026 budget proposed restructuring rental assistance and time limits, but Congress did not adopt the proposed block grant in the spending measures discussed by FactCheck.org. Draft housing rules reported by ProPublica were not final rules at the time of that report and should not be presented as enacted policy.

What can responsibly be said about the election? Project 2025 was a visible political issue and a policy blueprint associated with influential people in the conservative coalition. It is not sound to claim it “won the election” without voter-level evidence isolating its influence. Voters' motivations were multiple; the election result is not blanket approval of every item in the blueprint.

Sources: FactCheck.org: guide to Project 2025 · FactCheck.org: implementation and overlap · FactCheck.org: Medicaid and HUD policy · Congressional Research Service: health provisions in P.L. 119-21 · KFF: Medicaid work requirements tracker (updated Nov. 13, 2026).

06 / Address the financial costs

A prototype-first cost model

The first goal is not to maximize spend or claim AI made the work free. It is to reduce irreversible cost while testing whether the experience is useful, accessible, clear and worth continuing.

Cost categories to record before scaling
CostCurrent approachWhat must be measured
DevelopmentExisting repositories, static hosting and owner-led AI-assisted workActual tool charges, founder time, review and rework hours
Hosting & distributionPublic portfolio, GitHub Pages and VercelIncremental charges, bandwidth, service limits and domain costs
Rights & source materialsVerified links/assets; fan prototype disclosedPermissions, licenses, clearance, commissioned work and credit
Commerce & fulfillmentExisting store link; no new inventory created by demoPlatform/payment fees, production, shipping, taxes, returns and margin
Accessibility & QASemantic controls, keyboard paths, responsive review and deploymentManual testing, assistive technology/device coverage and fixes
PromotionOrganic, channel-specific invitations; no paid campaign assumedCreative time, approved paid spend and attributable outcomes

No audited total cost, saved-hours claim, revenue, conversion rate or profit is established. Before paid expansion, produce a budget with cash costs, time, unit economics, break-even and a stop rule. Reuse available assets and infrastructure; don't buy inventory or commission work to prove a link works.
